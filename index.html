What is Phishing?

Phishing is a type of cyberattack where criminals impersonate legitimate organizations, colleagues, or trusted entities to trick individuals into revealing sensitive information — such as passwords, credit card numbers, Social Security numbers, or corporate credentials.

The term "phishing" is a play on "fishing" — attackers cast a wide net of deceptive messages, hoping victims will "bite." These attacks typically arrive via email, but also use text messages, phone calls, social media, and even fake websites.

⚠️
Why It Matters: According to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), phishing was the most reported cybercrime in 2023, with over 298,000 complaints. The Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG) observed nearly 5 million phishing attacks in 2023 — the worst year on record.

How Phishing Works

1

Reconnaissance

The attacker researches the target — gathering email addresses, job titles, interests, and organizational details from social media, company websites, and data breaches.

2

Crafting the Lure

A convincing message is created that mimics a trusted source — an email from IT, a shipping notification, a bank alert, or a message from a colleague. Attackers clone real templates and domains.

3

Delivery

The phishing message is sent to the target via email, SMS, social media, or other communication channels. Some attackers send millions of messages; others target a single person.

4

Exploitation

The victim clicks a malicious link, opens an infected attachment, or enters credentials on a fake website. Malware may be installed, or credentials are harvested in real time.

5

Data Exfiltration

The attacker uses stolen credentials or malware access to steal data, move laterally within the network, deploy ransomware, or commit financial fraud.

02

Types of Phishing Attacks

Phishing has evolved into many specialized forms, each exploiting different communication channels and trust relationships.

📧

Email Phishing

The most common form. Attackers send mass emails impersonating banks, tech companies, or government agencies. These emails contain urgent calls to action — "Your account has been compromised!" — with links to fake login pages. Often uses spoofed sender addresses and near-identical branding.

Most Common
🎯

Spear Phishing

Highly targeted attacks directed at specific individuals or organizations. Attackers research their victims thoroughly — using LinkedIn profiles, company org charts, and social media — to craft personalized, believable messages. Far more dangerous than mass phishing because of their convincing nature.

High Risk
🐋

Whaling

A sub-type of spear phishing targeting C-suite executives and senior leadership — CEOs, CFOs, and board members. Whaling emails often appear to be legal subpoenas, tax notices, or high-stakes business communications. A successful whaling attack can compromise entire organizations.

Executive Target
📱

Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Phishing via text messages (SMS). Attackers send messages that appear to be from banks, delivery services (FedEx, UPS), or government agencies like the IRS. These texts include shortened URLs that redirect to credential-harvesting sites or trigger malware downloads.

Growing Threat
📞

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Phone-based phishing where attackers call victims posing as bank representatives, tech support agents, or law enforcement. AI-generated voice cloning has made vishing far more convincing — attackers can now mimic the voice of a CEO or family member using just a few seconds of sample audio.

AI-Enhanced
🌐

Clone Phishing

The attacker creates a near-identical copy of a legitimate email that the victim previously received — replacing legitimate links or attachments with malicious ones. Because the victim recognizes the email format, they're more likely to trust it. Often claims to be a "resend" or "updated version."

Deceptive
🔍

Search Engine Phishing

Attackers create fake websites optimized for search engines (SEO poisoning) or purchase ads that appear above legitimate search results. Users searching for banking sites, software downloads, or customer support are redirected to convincing clones that harvest credentials.

Emerging
💬

Angler Phishing

Attackers create fake customer service accounts on social media platforms (Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram) and intercept customer complaints directed at real companies. They respond with malicious links disguised as "support pages" or ask victims to provide account details via DM.

How to Recognize Phishing

Learn the red flags that reveal a phishing attempt — even the most convincing ones.

From: security-alert@amaz0n-verify.com 🚩
To: you@yourcompany.com
Subject: ⚠️ URGENT: Your Account Will Be Suspended in 24 Hours! 🚩

Dear Valued Costumer, 🚩

We have detected unusual activity on your Amazon account. Your account will be permanently suspended unless you verify your identity within 24 hours.

Please click the link below to confirm your account information:

https://amaz0n-verify.com/secure/login 🚩

If you do not verify within 24 hours, your account and all associated data will be permanently deleted. 🚩

Thank you,
Amazon Security Team

⬆️ Hover over 🚩 flags to see what's wrong

🚩 Phishing Red Flags Checklist

⊘
Suspicious sender address

Check the actual email address, not just the display name. Look for misspelled domains, extra characters, or domains that don't match the company (e.g., @amaz0n-verify.com vs @amazon.com).

⊘
Urgency and pressure tactics

Phrases like "Act immediately," "Your account will be suspended," or "You have 24 hours" are designed to override your critical thinking. Legitimate companies rarely impose immediate deadlines via email.

⊘
Generic greetings

"Dear Customer" or "Dear User" instead of your actual name. Companies you do business with typically know your name and use it in communications.

⊘
Spelling and grammar errors

Professional organizations have editorial standards. Multiple typos, awkward phrasing, or obvious grammatical mistakes are strong indicators of phishing — though AI-generated phishing is reducing this signal.

⊘
Suspicious links and URLs

Hover over links before clicking. Check for misspelled domains, unusual subdomains (e.g., amazon.evil-site.com), or URLs that use URL shorteners to hide the destination.

⊘
Unexpected attachments

Be cautious of .exe, .zip, .docm, .xlsm, or .js files. Even PDFs and Office documents can contain malicious macros or embedded links. Never open attachments you didn't expect.

⊘
Requests for sensitive information

Legitimate organizations never ask for passwords, Social Security numbers, or full credit card details via email. If in doubt, contact the organization directly through their official website or phone number.

⊘
Too-good-to-be-true offers

"You've won a $1,000 gift card!" or "Congratulations on your lottery prize!" If you didn't enter a contest, you didn't win. These offers are bait.

04

Real-World Phishing Case Studies

These major incidents prove that phishing can compromise anyone — from tech giants to government agencies.

2013–2015

Google & Facebook Invoice Scam

Loss: $121 Million

Lithuanian national Evaldas Rimasauskas orchestrated one of the largest phishing scams in history. He impersonated Quanta Computer, a real Taiwanese hardware manufacturer that does business with both Google and Facebook.

Method: Rimasauskas created a fake company with the same name as Quanta Computer, opened bank accounts in its name in Latvia and Cyprus, and sent fraudulent invoices with forged contracts and letters to Google and Facebook's accounting departments over two years.
Impact: Google paid approximately $23 million and Facebook paid approximately $98 million before the scheme was uncovered. Both companies eventually recovered most of the funds.
Lesson: Even the most sophisticated tech companies are vulnerable to business email compromise (BEC). Always verify payment requests through a secondary channel — especially for large amounts.
2014

Sony Pictures Hack

Devastating Data Breach

In November 2014, a group identifying itself as "Guardians of Peace" (GOP) — later attributed to North Korea's Lazarus Group — launched a devastating cyberattack against Sony Pictures Entertainment.

Method: The attackers used spear-phishing emails sent to Sony employees, disguised as messages from Apple regarding Apple ID verification. These emails contained links to a fake website that harvested employee credentials, giving the attackers access to Sony's internal network.
Impact: Over 100 terabytes of data were stolen, including unreleased films, employee Social Security numbers, salary data, private emails between executives, and sensitive business plans. The leaked emails caused massive reputational damage. Estimated cost: over $100 million.
Lesson: A single employee clicking a phishing link can compromise an entire organization. Comprehensive security awareness training and network segmentation are essential defenses.
2020

Twitter Bitcoin Scam

High-Profile Account Hijacking

On July 15, 2020, the Twitter accounts of Barack Obama, Joe Biden, Elon Musk, Bill Gates, Apple, Uber, and other high-profile entities were simultaneously hijacked to promote a Bitcoin scam.

Method: A 17-year-old hacker and accomplices used phone-based spear-phishing (vishing) to target Twitter employees. They called employees posing as IT staff, directing them to a fake internal VPN login page. Once they obtained employee credentials, they accessed Twitter's internal admin tools to take over verified accounts.
Impact: The scam collected approximately $120,000 in Bitcoin. More critically, it exposed a massive security vulnerability at one of the world's most influential social media platforms. Twitter's stock dropped, and the company faced intense regulatory scrutiny.
Lesson: Internal tools must be protected with multi-factor authentication and zero-trust architecture. Phone-based social engineering (vishing) is a serious and often underestimated threat vector.
2016

Democratic National Committee (DNC) Breach

Geopolitical Impact

Russian intelligence operatives (APT28/Fancy Bear) targeted the DNC and Clinton campaign chairman John Podesta's email account during the 2016 U.S. presidential election.

Method: Podesta received a spear-phishing email disguised as a Google security alert warning him that someone had tried to access his account. The email contained a "Change Password" link that led to a fake Google login page. An IT aide who reviewed the email mistakenly told Podesta it was "a legitimate email" (later claiming he had meant to write "illegitimate").
Impact: Over 50,000 emails from Podesta's account were stolen and published by WikiLeaks. The breach had significant implications for the 2016 U.S. presidential election and U.S.-Russia relations.
Lesson: Even a single misinterpreted advisory can lead to catastrophic results. Organizations must establish clear, verified procedures for handling potential phishing reports. Never click links in security alerts — go directly to the service.

05

Social Engineering Tactics

Phishing exploits human psychology, not just technology. Understanding these psychological manipulation techniques is critical to defending against them.

⏰

Urgency & Scarcity

Attackers create artificial time pressure to force hasty decisions. Messages like "Your account will be locked in 1 hour" or "Only 2 spots left — claim your reward now!" exploit the psychological principle that people make worse decisions under time pressure.

Example: "FINAL WARNING: Your PayPal account has been limited. Restore access within 24 hours or your funds will be frozen permanently."
Defense: Pause and take a breath. Legitimate organizations give reasonable timeframes and don't threaten permanent consequences via email. Verify through official channels.
👔

Authority

People tend to comply with requests from perceived authority figures without questioning them. Phishers impersonate CEOs, government officials, IT administrators, or law enforcement to leverage this built-in compliance tendency.

Example: "Hi, this is the CEO. I'm in a meeting and need you to process a wire transfer of $45,000 to this account urgently. Don't discuss this with anyone — it's confidential."
Defense: Always verify unusual requests through a separate communication channel. Call the person directly using a known phone number — not one provided in the email.
😨

Fear & Intimidation

Fear shuts down rational thinking. Phishers threaten account closure, legal action, arrest, or financial penalties to trigger a fear response that overrides critical evaluation of the message.

Example: "The IRS has identified a discrepancy in your tax return. Failure to respond within 48 hours will result in legal proceedings and potential arrest."
Defense: Remember that the IRS, law enforcement, and courts communicate via official mail — not email or text. Any message threatening immediate arrest is almost certainly a scam.
🤔

Curiosity

Humans are naturally curious, and attackers exploit this by sending intriguing or mysterious messages. Subject lines like "You won't believe what your coworker said about you" or "Photos from last night's event" are designed to compel clicks.

Example: "Someone shared a document with you: 'Q4 Salary Adjustments - Confidential.' Click here to view."
Defense: If you didn't expect a document or message, verify with the supposed sender through a separate channel before clicking any links.
🤝

Trust & Familiarity

Attackers exploit existing trust relationships by compromising a colleague's email account and using it to send phishing messages to their contacts. Since the email comes from a known, trusted address, victims are far less likely to question it.

Example: An email from your actual manager's compromised account: "Hey, can you review this invoice and process the payment? I'm out of office but it's urgent. Thanks!"
Defense: Be suspicious of any unexpected request involving money, credentials, or sensitive data — even from known contacts. Verify verbally.
🎁

Reciprocity & Reward

People feel obligated to reciprocate when they receive something — even something small. Phishers offer free gift cards, discounts, or "exclusive" access to create a sense of obligation that motivates the victim to comply with requests.

Example: "Congratulations! As a valued customer, you've been selected for a $500 Amazon gift card. Claim your reward by verifying your account details here."
Defense: If you didn't enter a contest or promotion, you didn't win anything. Free offers that require personal information are almost always scams.

Best Practices: Do's and Don'ts

Follow these guidelines to protect yourself and your organization from phishing attacks.

✅

DO

Verify the Sender

Always check the full email address — not just the display name. Contact the sender through a known, separate channel if anything seems unusual.

Hover Before You Click

On desktop, hover your cursor over links to preview the actual URL. On mobile, long-press links. Ensure the domain matches the expected organization.

Enable Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

Use MFA on all accounts — especially email, banking, and work accounts. Even if credentials are stolen, MFA provides a critical second layer of defense.

Report Suspicious Emails

Use your organization's phishing report button or forward suspicious emails to your IT/security team. Reporting helps protect the entire organization.

Keep Software Updated

Install security updates promptly. Patches fix vulnerabilities that phishing payloads exploit. Enable automatic updates where possible.

Use a Password Manager

Password managers generate unique, strong passwords for every account and won't auto-fill on fake websites — providing an extra layer of phishing detection.

Go Direct to Websites

Instead of clicking links in emails, open a new browser tab and type the organization's URL directly or use a saved bookmark.

❌

DON'T

Click Links in Unexpected Emails

Never click links in emails you didn't expect — even if they appear to come from trusted senders. Accounts can be compromised and used to send phishing.

Open Unknown Attachments

Never open attachments from unknown senders, or unexpected attachments from known senders. Malware is commonly delivered via Word docs, PDFs, and ZIP files.

Share Passwords or Sensitive Data via Email

No legitimate organization will ever ask for your password, Social Security number, or full credit card number via email, text, or phone.

Trust Display Names Alone

Email display names can be set to anything — "Google Support" can mask the address phisher@evil.com. Always check the actual email address.

Use the Same Password Everywhere

If one account is compromised, attackers will try that password on every other service (credential stuffing). Use unique passwords for every account.

Respond to Unsolicited Requests

Don't reply to unexpected emails asking you to verify account details, update payment information, or confirm personal data. Go to the official website instead.

Ignore Red Flags

Trust your instincts. If something feels off — unusual tone, unexpected urgency, or a too-good-to-be-true offer — it probably is. When in doubt, report it.
